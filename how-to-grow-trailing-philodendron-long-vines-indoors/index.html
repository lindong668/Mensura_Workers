How to Grow a Trailing Philodendron: Long Vines Indoors

Published 2026-11-24 10:00 · Elena Marsh · Photo sources

A trailing philodendron trails along the shelf, but the vines are thinning at the base, leaves are smaller than they used to be, and bare stems show between the growth. These vining aroids are fast growers when light and watering suit them; legginess and shrinking leaves usually come from a darker spot or a root-bound pot. This guide covers light, support, watering, feeding and pruning to keep the vines long and leafy.

What You’ll Need

Everything below is written for a plant kept in a normal living room. For this guide, the useful kit is short and ordinary:

  • A hanging basket or a trellis/moss pole the vines can climb.
  • Bright indirect light, away from direct midday sun on the leaves.
  • A pot with drainage and an emptyable saucer, or a basket lined to hold mix.
  • Loose free-draining aroid or ordinary houseplant mix.
  • Clean scissors for trimming and, if wanted, taking cuttings.

Safety note. Wear gloves if you are trimming rotted tissue, and keep tools away from children and pets. Work on a stable surface, and keep pets and children away from cut material.

The shortest route is to check the clues below first, then change one thing at a time.

  • Leaves shrink along the vine and gaps appear between nodes as growth stretches toward light.
  • The pot feels tight and roots may circle the surface or emerge from the drainage hole.
  • The mix stays damp for days and the lower leaves yellow rather than just ageing.

Step-by-Step Guide

  1. Step 1 — Move it into bright indirect light

    A few metres from a window, or behind a sheer curtain, gives the vining growth the brightness it needs without scorching the leaves. Low light is the most common reason vines elongate with small, widely spaced leaves.

  2. Step 2 — Give the vines a direction to grow

    Hang the basket so stems trail freely, or tie climbing stems to a moss pole or trellis. Vines that reach for support grow larger leaves than those left sprawled on a shelf.

  3. Step 3 — Water when the top few centimetres dry

    Feel the mix before each drink. Philodendron roots like to dry down between waterings; soak thoroughly and let surplus drain, rather than keeping the mix permanently damp.

  4. Step 4 — Repot when the roots crowd the pot

    If roots circle the root ball or poke through the hole, move to a one-size-larger pot with fresh loose mix. A bound root ball limits leaf size however much you feed.

  5. Step 5 — Feed lightly through the growing season

    Dilute an ordinary liquid houseplant feed to half strength once a month in spring and summer. Keep it off dry soil, and stop feeding in winter.

  6. Step 6 — Prune leggy stems to encourage branching

    Cut a long bare vine back to a healthy node; the remaining stem usually sideshoots. Trimmings root easily in water or soil, which is also how you fill a sparse basket.

Why This Works

Trailing philodendrons are understory vines that climb tree trunks in nature, using supports to reach brighter air and producing bigger leaves as they climb. In a home, a lack of support and light leaves them stretching along the floor or shelf, where growth is weaker.

Their roots are adapted to loose, open bark and leaf litter, so compacted wet soil chokes them far faster than a short dry spell. Keeping the mix aerated and letting it dry between drinks mirrors that open-rooted habit.

What to Avoid

Four responses undo the work above; avoid these while the condition is still present, not only after it clears.

  • Do not keep the soil soggy; philodendron roots rot in perpetually wet, compacted mix.
  • Do not place it in direct midday sun, which burns the soft foliage.
  • Do not overpot; an oversized vessel holds damp soil the small roots cannot use.
  • Do not cut back only the leaf tips; pruning at a node is what triggers new side growth.

Daily Care Tips

Once the immediate problem is handled, these small habits keep the plant from drifting back into the same situation.

  • Rotate the basket monthly so trailing vines fill evenly on all sides.
  • Wipe dust from the leaves so the vine can photosynthesise efficiently.
  • Pinch the growing tips once in early summer to encourage branching.
  • Check new leaf size when judging whether conditions are right.

FAQs

How do I make my philodendron vine longer?

Bright indirect light and a support do most of the work; keep the root zone airy and watered on a dry-down cycle, and let the vines grow without hard pruning.

Why are the leaves getting smaller?

Usually low light, a root-bound pot, or both. Leaves shrink when the plant stretches for light and when crowded roots cannot supply enough water and nutrients.

Can philodendron climb a wall?

It can be trained onto a trellis or wall-mounted support with ties; bare wall contact alone will not make it attach the way an ivy would.

Is trailing philodendron toxic to pets?

Like many aroids, it contains irritant sap that causes mouth and stomach discomfort if chewed. Keep vines out of reach of nibbling pets.

Final Thoughts

Bright indirect light, a support, and an airy root zone make the vines long and leafy; legginess usually means a darker spot or a root-bound pot rather than a watering problem.

Where this guide applies

These steps assume a hanging basket or pot with drainage. In a decorative cachepot with no hole, moisture must be checked carefully and surplus water removed after each soak.
