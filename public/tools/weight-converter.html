Pounds, stone and the UK habit

The pound is defined as exactly 0.45359237 kg. The stone (14 lb = 6.35029318 kg) has no official SI standing but remains the everyday unit for human body weight in the UK and Ireland.

A stone is a compound unit in practice: UK speakers rarely use decimals, saying "11 stone 4" to mean 11 × 14 + 4 = 158 lb (about 71.7 kg). If you are converting a figure written that way, multiply the first number by 14, add the second, and convert the total — converting "11" and "4" separately gives the wrong answer.

Luggage and parcel limits: the conversion that costs money

Airline baggage allowances are the most common real-world reason to convert between kilograms and pounds, and the two figures on the tag are rarely the same number. A standard economy checked allowance of 23 kg is 50.71 lb, which airlines round down and print as "50 lb" — so a bag weighing exactly 50.0 lb is fine, while one at 23.5 kg (51.8 lb) is not. The heavier allowance of 32 kg is 70.55 lb, printed as "70 lb".

The safe direction is always to convert from the airline's stated figure, then weigh to the stricter of the two. Because 50 lb is 22.68 kg, a limit printed as "23 kg" is very slightly stricter than one printed as "50 lb" even though they describe the same policy.

Which unit each country actually uses

Body weight is reported in pounds in the United States, in stone-and-pounds in the UK and Ireland, and in kilograms almost everywhere else — including countries that still use imperial units for distance, such as the United States' own medical records, where kilograms are standard in clinical settings. This is why converting a UK figure requires two steps (stone → pounds → kg) rather than one.

For anything with a weight limit printed on it — luggage, parcel services, gym equipment, vehicle payloads — the figure is usually given in the unit of whichever market the document was printed for. Convert before comparing, rather than assuming two limits are equivalent because they look close.

Mass versus weight

Strictly, kilograms measure mass and newtons measure force (weight). A 70 kg person has the same mass on the Moon but weighs about a sixth as much. In everyday and commercial usage "weight" in kilograms or pounds means mass, and that is what this tool converts. Laboratory work that genuinely needs force should convert to newtons instead.
