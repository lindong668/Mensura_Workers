Mean is not always the honest summary

The classic distortion. In a room of ten people where nine earn $40k and one earns $1M, the mean income is about $136k — a figure nobody in the room earns. The median is $40k. When data is skewed by a few extreme values, the median describes the typical case and the mean describes the arithmetic.

This is why income, house prices and response times are conventionally reported as medians: they are resistant to outliers. Use the mean when values cluster symmetrically and you care about the total; use the median when outliers exist and you care about the typical case.

Sample versus population standard deviation

Standard deviation measures spread. There are two versions, differing only in the divisor:

  • Population divides by n — use when your data is the entire group of interest.
  • Sample divides by n−1 — use when your data is a sample meant to estimate a larger group.

The n−1 version is Bessel's correction, and it compensates for the fact that a sample's spread tends to slightly underestimate the population's. It is what most spreadsheet STDEV functions and statistics courses default to, and it is what this tool reports.

Reading standard deviation

For roughly bell-shaped data, about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean and about 95% within two. If your data is strongly skewed or has extreme outliers, that rule of thumb breaks down — check the median and range first.

On mode

The mode is the most frequent value, and it is the only one of these statistics that works for categories as well as numbers (most common colour, most frequent error code). It is also the least stable: small changes in data can change the mode, and many datasets have no repeated value at all — in which case there is no mode.
