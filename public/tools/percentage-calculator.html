Percent versus percentage point

The distinction that financial reporting gets wrong constantly. If a rate rises from 4% to 6%, that is a rise of 2 percentage points — but a 50% relative increase ((6−4)/4). Both descriptions are correct and they are not interchangeable. "Interest rates rose 2%" and "rose by 2 percentage points" describe completely different changes.

Gains and losses are not symmetric

A 50% loss requires a 100% gain to get back to the starting point. If $100 falls 50% to $50, returning to $100 needs a 100% rise on $50. This asymmetry is why long-run investment returns are usually quoted as an annualised figure rather than as the arithmetic average of yearly percentage moves.

Successive percentages multiply

Two consecutive 10% increases do not total 20%. The second applies to an already-increased base: 100 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 121, a 21% rise. Likewise a 20% discount followed by an extra 10% off gives 100 × 0.80 × 0.90 = 72, which is 28% off, not 30%.

Watch the base

Percentage change always divides by the original value, which makes the choice of baseline decisive. A change from 50 to 100 is +100%; from 100 back to 50 is −50%. Same distance, different percentages, because the denominator changed.
