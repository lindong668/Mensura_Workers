Temperature Converter

Convert between Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin and Rankine. Temperature is the rare quantity where conversion needs an offset as well as a factor — a point that trips up spreadsheets and quick mental maths alike.

Why temperature is not a simple ratio

Length, mass and volume all share a true zero: zero metres means no length, so you can convert by multiplying alone. Temperature scales have arbitrary zeros. 0 °C is the freezing point of water, not the absence of heat. So every conversion needs both a scale factor and an offset:

  • °F = °C × 9/5 + 32
  • K = °C + 273.15
  • °R = °F + 459.67

This is also why "20 °C is twice as hot as 10 °C" is meaningless — the statement depends on which zero you picked. It is true in Kelvin (293.15 K is not double 283.15 K either) but never in Celsius.

The one point where Celsius and Fahrenheit agree

−40° = −40°. It is the only temperature at which the Celsius and Fahrenheit readings are identical, because −40 is where the two linear scales cross. Useful as a sanity check: any converter that does not return −40 for both at that input is wrong.

Kelvin has no negative values

Kelvin starts at absolute zero (0 K = −273.15 °C), the theoretical point of minimum thermal energy. A negative Kelvin result signals an input error or a physically impossible state, not a valid temperature. Rankine is the absolute counterpart to Fahrenheit and is used in some US engineering thermodynamics contexts.

Fahrenheit's 180-degree decision

Fahrenheit set the freezing point of water at 32 and body temperature near 96, giving 180 degrees between freezing and boiling — a number chosen because it divides cleanly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 12. The scale was later recalibrated so boiling sits at exactly 212 and normal body temperature settled at 98.6 °F.
