Cooking Converter

Convert cooking measures across cups, spoons and millilitres — and convert volume to weight using per-ingredient density. Handles the fact that a cup is 240 mL in the US, 200 mL in Japan and 250 mL in Australia.

A cup is three different sizes

The most common recipe failure. The US legal cup is 240 mL. The Japanese cup is 200 mL. The Australian cup is 250 mL. A recipe developed with one and measured with another is off by up to 20% in every ingredient — enough to change a cake's texture entirely. Australian tablespoons are also larger: 20 mL versus the US 15 mL.

A second trap inside the US itself. Two different US sets exist and they must not be mixed:

  • US legal — cup 240 mL, tablespoon 15 mL, teaspoon 5 mL. This is what nutrition labels use, and it is internally consistent: 1 cup = 16 tbsp = 48 tsp.
  • US customary — cup 236.588 mL, tablespoon 14.787 mL, teaspoon 4.929 mL. This is what older cookbooks mean, and it is also internally consistent: 1 cup = 16 tbsp = 48 tsp.

Combining a legal cup with customary spoons gives 16.23 tablespoons per cup — the kind of 1.4% drift that quietly ruins a recipe that scales. Both sets are listed above; stay inside one.

Volume to weight needs density

Millilitres measure space; grams measure substance. Bridging them requires density, and cooking ingredients vary enormously. One US cup (240 mL) holds very different masses:

IngredientDensity (g/mL)Weight per US cup
Water1.00240 g
Milk1.03247 g
All-purpose flour0.53127 g
Granulated sugar0.85204 g
Butter0.911219 g
Honey1.42341 g
Rolled oats0.3584 g

Note the flour figure: 127 g, not 240 g. Assuming "a cup weighs 240 grams" because it works for water will badly over-flour a recipe.

Flour is the ingredient most worth weighing

Flour's apparent density depends entirely on how it is handled. Scooped straight from the bag, a cup can hold 140–150 g; spooned in and levelled, closer to 120 g. That is a 25% swing in the single most structurally important ingredient in baking, which is why experienced bakers weigh flour and treat cup measures as approximate.

Butter and the wrapper trick

Butter at 0.911 g/mL means one US stick (½ cup, 120 mL) weighs about 110 g — close enough to the familiar 113 g (4 oz) label that packaging is marked in tablespoons for convenience. Slight differences between salted and unsalted butter, and temperature-driven density changes, are why professional recipes specify weight.

Practical advice

  • Weigh dry ingredients, especially flour and cocoa.
  • Use volume only for liquids and for small spoon measures.
  • Identify which country's cup your recipe assumes before measuring.
  • Remember brown sugar is usually specified "packed" — its density in the table above reflects that.
