Best Potting Soil for Indoor Plants: Complete Buying Guide

Published 2026-09-21 07:19 · Elena Marsh · Photo sources

One bag of general-purpose compost is being used for a cactus, a monstera and an African violet, with poor results. Match the mix structure to the plant: airy bark for aroids, gritty mineral for succulents, fine but free-draining for thin-rooted plants. Work in this order; each step depends on the one before it, and skipping ahead usually means undoing it later.

What You’ll Need

For potting soil, the useful kit is short and ordinary:

  • Plain potting mix as a base — to keep the root zone airy and free-draining
  • Coarse bark or coco chips — to keep the root zone airy and free-draining
  • Pumice, perlite or coarse grit — to keep the root zone airy and free-draining
  • A pot with drainage — so water can drain freely and never sit under the roots
  • A scoop and a tub for blending — so water can drain freely and never sit under the roots

Safety note. Wear a mask when pouring dry perlite or fine mineral dust; avoid inhaling dust. Work on a stable surface, and keep pets and children away from cut material.

They separate a simple potting soil problem from one that needs a different response entirely:

  • Water runs through at a sensible speed and the pot does not stay wet for a week.
  • Roots are pale and firm when you eventually repot.
  • The mix does not compact into a dense sour mass.

Step-by-Step Guide

Work through the steps in order; each one builds on the previous, and you can stop once the plant is recovering.

  1. Step 1 — Ignore the marketing name and read the texture

    "Indoor", "cactus" and "orchid" labels vary widely; particle size and drainage are what actually matter.

  2. Step 2 — For aroids and large foliage plants, add coarse bark and perlite to a plain base

    Their thick roots need air pockets that a fine peat mix cannot hold.

  3. Step 3 — For succulents and cacti, go mostly mineral — roughly two parts grit to one part organic mix

    Fast drainage reproduces the dry pulse these plants are built around.

  4. Step 4 — For fine-rooted plants such as African violets, keep it light and airy but moisture-retentive

    Fine roots need both air and a steady, gentle moisture supply.

  5. Step 5 — Wet a sample in a spare pot and time how quickly it drains

    A two-minute test tells you more than a bag description.

  6. Step 6 — Sieve out fines and avoid water-retaining crystals indoors

    Fines settle into the air pores, and crystals keep the root zone wet long after the plant has finished drinking.

Why This Works

Container mixes work through their pore structure. Large pores drain by gravity and hold air; small pores hold water by capillary force. Roots need both. A dense fine mix eliminates the air-filled pores, while an entirely coarse mix dries too fast for thin-rooted plants. That is why a single bag cannot serve a cactus, a monstera and a violet equally well — the required ratio of coarse to fine particles differs.

That is also why the common "quick fix" tends to fail on potting soil: it treats the visible symptom rather than the condition producing it.

What to Avoid

These are the common mistakes that set the plant back; avoid them while the problem persists.

  • Do not use garden soil in a container.
  • Do not pack the mix down firmly.
  • Do not assume "cactus compost" is gritty enough without checking.
  • Do not reuse a mix that has gone sour or compacted.

Daily Care Tips

Once the immediate problem is handled, these small habits keep potting soil from drifting back into the same situation.

  • Store mix dry and sealed so it does not absorb humidity.
  • Match particle size to pot size.
  • Top-dress with grit where fungus gnats are a problem.
  • Feed separately; do not enrich the mix to solve a care problem.

FAQs

Can I use one mix for everything?

You can, but you will be fighting either rot or dryness. Blend by plant type instead.

Is perlite or bark better?

They do different jobs: bark adds structure for aroids, perlite and grit add drainage for succulents.

Why does shop cactus compost stay wet?

It is often still peat-heavy. Add extra pumice or coarse grit.

How often should I replace mix?

When it compacts, stays wet for days or smells sour — typically every couple of years, not on a calendar.

Final Thoughts

Buy for texture, blend for the plant type, and test drainage before you commit a whole collection.

Where this guide applies

These steps assume a plastic or glazed pot with a working drainage hole.
