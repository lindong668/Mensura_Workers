GCD & LCM Calculator

Compute the greatest common divisor and least common multiple of two or more positive integers. Both are found with Euclid's algorithm, which is fast even for very large numbers.

Separate with commas or spaces.

What they mean

  • GCD — the largest number that divides all of them exactly. GCD(12, 18) = 6.
  • LCM — the smallest number that all of them divide into. LCM(12, 18) = 36.

They are linked

GCD(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b. This identity holds for two numbers, which means computing one gives the other for free. It does not extend directly to three or more numbers — for those, the tool chains the operation pairwise.

Euclid's algorithm

Rather than factoring numbers (slow for large values), the GCD is found by repeated remainder: replace the pair (a, b) with (b, a mod b) until the remainder is zero. The last non-zero remainder is the GCD. It runs in a number of steps proportional to the number of digits, which is why it remains the standard method over two millennia after Euclid described it.

Where these show up

  • Simplifying fractions — divide numerator and denominator by their GCD.
  • Tiling and cutting — the GCD gives the largest square tile that fits a rectangle exactly.
  • Repeating cycles — the LCM tells you when two periodic events next coincide.
  • Gear ratios and scheduling — LCM determines when cycles realign.

When the GCD is 1

Two numbers whose GCD is 1 are called coprime or relatively prime — they share no factor other than 1. This matters because it is the point where simplification stops: a fraction such as 8/15 cannot be reduced, because GCD(8, 15) = 1.

Note that coprime numbers do not have to be prime themselves. 8 and 15 are both composite, yet nothing divides both. When numbers are coprime, their LCM is simply their product — 8 × 15 = 120 — which is the largest the LCM can ever be.

Worked example

GCD(48, 180): 180 mod 48 = 36; 48 mod 36 = 12; 36 mod 12 = 0. The last non-zero remainder is 12, so GCD = 12 and LCM = 48 × 180 ÷ 12 = 720.
